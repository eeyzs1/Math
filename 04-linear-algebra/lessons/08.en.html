Linear Algebra / LESSON 08

Inner Products, Projections, and Least Squares

Goal for this lesson: explain "Inner Products, Projections, and Least Squares" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Elementary algebra, the coordinate plane. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

The inner product u·v=Σuᵢvᵢ yields lengths ‖u‖=√(u·u) and perpendicularity u·v=0. The nearest point of a subspace is reached by orthogonal projection: the residual is perpendicular to the subspace. Least-squares data fitting is exactly a projection — the observation vector b is projected onto the space spanned by the predictor columns; the condition Aᵀ(b−Ax*)=0 expands into the normal equations AᵀAx*=Aᵀb.

A Derivation, Step by Step

The least-squares line through the points (1,2), (2,3), (3,5) is y=1/3+1.5x (the closed form appears in Lesson 06 of 10). Geometrically: b=(2,3,5) is projected onto the plane spanned by (1,1,1) and (1,2,3), and the residual is perpendicular to that plane.

Pause and Try It

Why must the residual be perpendicular to the whole 'fitting plane' rather than only to the predictor direction? Which notion of distance does 'closest' use?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Let p be the orthogonal projection of b onto the fitted subspace S, so e=b−p is perpendicular to S. For any q∈S, b−q=e+(p−q), hence ‖b−q‖²=‖e‖²+‖p−q‖²≥‖e‖², with equality only for q=p. This proves the nearest-point property in Euclidean distance. Orthogonality to the predictor column alone is insufficient: the residual must also be perpendicular to the constant column and every other fitted direction.
