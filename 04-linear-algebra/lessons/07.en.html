Linear Algebra / LESSON 07

Eigenvalues and Diagonalization

Goal for this lesson: explain "Eigenvalues and Diagonalization" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Elementary algebra, the coordinate plane. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Av=λv (v≠0) says that A does not change the direction of v and only scales it by λ; v is called an eigenvector and λ an eigenvalue. The eigenvalues are the roots of det(A−λI)=0; in the 2×2 case this is λ²−(trace)λ+det=0 — computable with nothing more than the quadratic formula from 02. With two independent eigenvectors as columns forming P, we get A=PDP⁻¹, and powers of A become powers of D: difference equations, vibrations, and the convergence of iterations are all read off from here.

A Derivation, Step by Step

A=[[3,1],[0,2]]: the characteristic polynomial is (3−λ)(2−λ), so λ=3,2. For λ=3, solving (A−3I)v=0 gives v=(1,0); for λ=2, v=(1,−1) — check: A(1,−1)=(2,−2)=2(1,−1).

Pause and Try It

Find the eigenvalues of the 90° rotation matrix [[0,−1],[1,0]], and explain why it has no real eigenvectors.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Trace 0 and determinant 1 give λ²+1=0, so λ=±i. A rotation preserves no real line direction, so over the reals there are no eigenvectors; the purely imaginary pair corresponds exactly to the oscillating solutions of the second-order equations in 11 — complex numbers here are not decoration but the algebraic notation for 'going in circles'.
