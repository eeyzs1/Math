Linear Algebra / LESSON 03

Matrices: Writing a Map as a Table

Goal for this lesson: explain "Matrices" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Elementary algebra, the coordinate plane. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A matrix is a table of numbers, but more importantly a notation for a map: Ax denotes the image of the vector x under a linear map, with linearity visible in A(x+y)=Ax+Ay and A(cx)=cAx. Matrix multiplication corresponds to composition of maps: first B, then A, is written AB — which is why matrix multiplication is generally not commutative. The identity matrix is the map that does nothing.

A Derivation, Step by Step

The matrix of a 90° counterclockwise rotation sends (1,0) to (0,1) and (0,1) to (−1,0), which as a table is [[0,−1],[1,0]]. Doing it twice is a 180° rotation, and the computation indeed yields −I, matching intuition.

Pause and Try It

For A=[[1,1],[0,1]], compute A(2,3), and describe what A does to the unit square and why its area is unchanged.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

A(2,3)=(2+3,3)=(5,3). The first column (1,0) stays in place while the second column becomes (1,1): the square is sheared into a parallelogram. det(A)=1, so the area is unchanged.
