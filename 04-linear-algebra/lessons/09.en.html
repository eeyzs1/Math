Linear Algebra / LESSON 09

A First Look at Tensors: Multilinear Forms

Goal for this lesson: explain "A First Look at Tensors" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Elementary algebra, the coordinate plane. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

On a finite-dimensional real vector space V, a bilinear form is linear in each argument separately; the Euclidean inner product is a positive-definite symmetric example. A real-valued function of k vectors that is separately linear is a covariant tensor of order k; tensors also include other types. An alternating form vanishes when two inputs coincide and changes sign when two inputs are swapped. In dimension two, alternating bilinear forms form a one-dimensional space: each is a multiple of the determinant, uniquely normalized by value 1 on the standard ordered basis.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Let B be alternating: B(u,u)=0 and B(u,v)=−B(v,u). Put c=B(e₁,e₂); linearity forces B(u,v)=c(u₁v₂−u₂v₁)=c·det[u v] — no matter how you build it, a two-dimensional alternating form cannot escape being a multiple of det.

Pause and Try It

Verify that B(u,v)=u₁v₂−u₂v₁ is antisymmetric, and explain why 'signed area' must be antisymmetric and linear in each column.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

B(v,u)=u₂v₁−u₁v₂=−B(u,v). Swapping the two columns reverses orientation, so the sign must change; scaling an edge scales the area proportionally, which is linearity in each column. These three axioms determine det uniquely — a characterization that, unlike the 'cross-multiplying diagonals' formula, transfers to higher dimensions and to multilinear algebra (see the 04 branch page for the route).
