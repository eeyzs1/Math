Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem

Goal for this lesson: explain "Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Fractions, equations, and basic proofs. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

The left cosets aH={ah: h∈H} of a subgroup H slice the group into equal-sized blocks: two cosets are either identical or disjoint, and multiplication by a is a bijection from H to aH. The number of blocks is called the index. Lagrange's theorem: the order of a finite group equals the subgroup order times the index; hence the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group, and so does the order of each element.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Take G=Z/6 (additive) and H={0,3}: the cosets are H, 1+H={1,4}, and 2+H={2,5} — three blocks with 2 elements each, and 6=3×2. Note that 3+H=H: different representatives can determine the same coset.

Pause and Try It

Prove that a group of prime order has no nontrivial subgroups, and deduce that it must be cyclic.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

In a group of prime order p, a subgroup's order must divide p, so it is 1 or p. Take any non-identity element a; the subgroup ⟨a⟩ contains a and is nontrivial, hence it equals the whole group: the group is generated by a and is cyclic. The proof of Fermat's Little Theorem in 03 reads out in exactly this framework.
