Rings, Ideals, and Quotient Rings

Goal for this lesson: explain "Rings, Ideals, and Quotient Rings" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Fractions, equations, and basic proofs. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A ring carries both addition and multiplication, with multiplication distributing over addition, as in Z and in polynomial rings. An ideal I is a subset that is a group under addition and "absorbs multiplication from outside" (rI⊆I); the set (n) of multiples of n is an ideal of Z. The quotient ring Z/nZ treats numbers "differing by n" as one and the same — exactly the algebraization of congruence arithmetic.

A Derivation, Step by Step

In Z/6Z, [2]+[3]=[5], while [2]·[3]=[6]=[0]: two nonzero elements multiply to zero (zero divisors), so Z/6 is not a field.

Pause and Try It

Why is Z/5 a field while Z/6 is not?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

A field requires every nonzero element to be invertible. In Z/5, [2]·[3]=[6]=[1], so every nonzero element has an inverse; in Z/6, [2]·[3]=[0], and if [2] were invertible it would follow that [3]=[0], a contradiction. The root cause is that 5 is prime and 6 is composite.
