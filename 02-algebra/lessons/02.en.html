Factoring and the Quadratic Formula

Goal for this lesson: explain "Factoring and the Quadratic Formula" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Fractions, equations, and basic proofs. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Expanding and factoring are inverse processes, and the tool behind both is the distributive law: a(b+c)=ab+ac. Workhorse patterns: factoring out a common factor, the difference of squares a²−b²=(a+b)(a−b), and the perfect squares (a±b)². Applying completing the square to the quadratic equation ax²+bx+c=0 yields the quadratic formula, and the discriminant Δ=b²−4ac determines the number of real roots. Here a, b, c are real and a≠0; otherwise the equation is not quadratic.

A Derivation, Step by Step

x²−5x+6: completing the square gives x²−5x=(x−5/2)²−25/4, and moving the constant term yields (x−5/2)²=1/4, so x=2 or 3 — consistent with the factorization (x−2)(x−3).

Pause and Try It

Solve 2x²−4x−6=0, and give the correspondence between the value of the discriminant and the number of real roots.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Divide both sides by 2: x²−2x−3=0, so (x−3)(x+1)=0 and x=3 or −1. Here Δ=(−4)²−4×2×(−6)=64>0, so two distinct real roots; Δ=0 would give one double root; Δ<0 gives no real roots, with the solutions living in the complex numbers (the next lesson).

Self-check: Is the conclusion correct? Are the conditions stated? Can you explain the key steps without looking at the example? If you only recalled the answer, redo the lesson.
