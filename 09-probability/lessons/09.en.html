Probability Theory / LESSON 09

From Random Walks to Brownian Motion

Goal for this lesson: explain "From Random Walks to Brownian Motion" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Fractions and sets; later lessons require analysis. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Shrink the step size and thicken the step count of a random walk; the limiting path is continuous yet 'kinked' everywhere — Brownian motion. The mathematical skeleton: an n-step ±1 walk has position variance n; with step size δ and 1/δ² steps per unit time, the variance stays (1/δ²)×δ²=1 — under this scaling limit the distribution at each instant is normal (Lessons 05 and 07), while the path is continuous yet differentiable nowhere. In a stochastic differential equation dX=b dt+σ dW, the σ dW term is precisely noise of this √t magnitude.

A Derivation, Step by Step

A 100-step ±1 walk has position standard deviation 10=√100; 10,000 steps give 100. The longer the time, the wider the spread — but it grows only like √t, a square root slower than the t growth of deterministic quantities.

Pause and Try It

Change the walk to step size δ with 1/δ² steps per unit time: what is the variance after one unit of time? What does this say about how noise accumulates over time?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The variance is (1/δ²)·δ²=1, constant; after t units of time it is t. Noise accumulates like √t, not t — the origin of 'volatility × √time' in financial models, and of the different orders of magnitude between the random term and the drift term (which accumulates like t).
