Probability Theory / LESSON 08

Random Walks and Markov Chains

Goal for this lesson: explain "Random Walks and Markov Chains" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Fractions and sets; later lessons require analysis. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

For a Markov chain, conditional on the current state, the next-state distribution does not depend on the earlier history; a fair ±1 random walk is an example. A time-homogeneous chain has transition matrix P with row sums 1 and k-step matrix Pᵏ. A finite irreducible aperiodic chain converges from any initial distribution to its unique stationary distribution. This is not true for every Markov chain: a deterministic two-state alternation keeps oscillating.

A Derivation, Step by Step

A one-dimensional walk returns to the origin in 4 steps: two rights and two lefts give C(4,2)=6 paths, with probability 6/16=0.375. Pólya's theorem: one- and two-dimensional walks return to the origin with probability 1; in three dimensions only about 0.34 — the higher the dimension, the easier it is to get lost.

Pause and Try It

A two-state sunny/rainy chain: sun→rain with probability 0.3, rain→sun with probability 0.4. In the long run, what fraction of days are sunny?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The stationary distribution satisfies π_sunny=0.7π_sunny+0.4π_rainy and π_sunny+π_rainy=1, giving π_sunny=4/7≈57%. Starting from sun or from rain, the sunny fraction approaches this number within days — it is precisely the left eigenvector of the transition matrix for the eigenvalue 1 (eigenvalues: see Lesson 07 of 04).
