Probability Theory / LESSON 05

Common Distributions: Binomial, Geometric, and Normal

Goal for this lesson: explain "Common Distributions" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Fractions and sets; later lessons require analysis. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Discrete: the binomial distribution Bin(n,p) counts the successes in n independent trials, P(X=k)=C(n,k)pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, with E=np and Var=np(1−p); the geometric distribution counts the number of trials until the first success, P(T=k)=(1−p)^(k−1)p, with E=1/p. Continuous: the normal distribution N(μ,σ²) has the bell curve as its density, with about 68% and 95% of the mass within ±1σ and ±2σ; after standardizing, (X−μ)/σ, any distribution can be compared with the standard normal N(0,1) — the stage for the central limit theorem is thereby set. Standardization changes the mean and variance to 0 and 1 when they exist with positive variance; it does not make an arbitrary distribution normal. An exactly standard normal result requires X itself to be normal. The geometric distribution here has 0<p≤1 and counts trials starting at 1.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Bin(10,0.5) (Interactive Lab 3): E=5, Var=2.5, and P(X≥8)=(45+10+1)/1024≈0.055. Waiting for the first six with a die: P(T=1)=1/6≈0.167, P(T=3)=(5/6)²×(1/6)≈0.116, and E=6.

Pause and Try It

How does the probability of 'first success at trial k' change with k? After 'three rolls without a six', is the expected further wait still 6?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

P(T=k) decreases with k: a long wait means poor luck so far. But each roll remains an independent trial, and the distribution of the remaining wait is the same as at the start (memorylessness), so the expectation is still 6. Distributions upgrade 'the probability of one event' into 'the shape of an entire random phenomenon'.
