Number Theory / LESSON 05

Bézout and Modular Inverses: Diophantine Equations

Goal for this lesson: explain "Bézout and Modular Inverses" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Integers, algebra, and proofs. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Bézout identity: there exist integers x,y with ax+by=gcd(a,b), obtained by back-substituting layer by layer through the Euclidean algorithm. Corollary: ax+by=c has integer solutions if and only if gcd(a,b) divides c. Congruence version: ax≡b (mod m) has a solution if and only if gcd(a,m) divides b; when gcd(a,m)=1, a has an inverse modulo m.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Solve 12x+30y=6: Euclid gives 30=2×12+6, i.e. 6=30−2×12, a particular solution (x,y)=(−2,1). The homogeneous solutions are (5t,−2t), so the general solution is (−2+5t, 1−2t).

Pause and Try It

Find the inverse of 7 modulo 31, and solve 3t≡1 (mod 5) — explain the role this plays in the Chinese Remainder Theorem in the next lesson.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

31=4×7+3 and 7=2×3+1; back-substitution gives 1=7−2×3=7−2(31−4×7)=9×7−2×31, so 7⁻¹≡9 (mod 31), and indeed 63=2×31+1. Also 3⁻¹ mod 5=2 (3×2=6≡1) — "dividing by the coefficient" when solving a system of congruences is really multiplying by such an inverse.
