Number Theory / LESSON 04

Fermat's Little Theorem and Euler's Theorem

Goal for this lesson: explain "Fermat's Little Theorem and Euler's Theorem" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Integers, algebra, and proofs. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Fermat's Little Theorem: if p is prime and p does not divide a, then a^(p−1)≡1 (mod p). Euler generalized it to a^φ(n)≡1 (mod n) (with the requirement gcd(a,n)=1), where φ(n) counts how many of 1..n are coprime to n. Proof idea: the residue classes coprime to n form a group under multiplication, of order φ(n); raising an element to the order of the group necessarily returns the identity (the order of an element divides the order of the group — the lesson on cosets and Lagrange's theorem in 02). These theorems are also the heart of the public-key cryptosystem RSA; see 14, Lesson 05.

A Derivation, Step by Step

For p=5, 2⁴=16≡1 (mod 5), hence 2¹⁰⁰=(2⁴)²⁵≡1. The 7⁴≡1 (mod 10) of the congruence lesson is explained too: φ(10)=4.

Pause and Try It

Find the remainder when 3²⁰⁰ is divided by 7.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

p=7, so Fermat gives 3⁶≡1 (mod 7). Writing 200=6×33+2, 3²⁰⁰≡3²=9≡2 (mod 7). Exponents reduce by multiples of p−1, and the cycle length of the powers divides the order of the group.

Self-check: Is the conclusion correct? Are the conditions stated? Can you explain the key steps without looking at the example? If you only recalled the answer, redo the lesson.
