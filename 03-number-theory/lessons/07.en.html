Number Theory / LESSON 07

Quadratic Residues: The Arithmetic of Being a Square

Goal for this lesson: explain "Quadratic Residues" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Integers, algebra, and proofs. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Let p be an odd prime and p not divide a. Here a is a nonzero quadratic residue modulo p if x²≡a (mod p) is solvable; zero is treated separately. Exactly half the nonzero classes are squares, since x and −x are distinct and give the same square. Euler's criterion gives a^((p−1)/2)≡1 for a residue and ≡−1 for a nonresidue. For distinct odd primes p,q, the Legendre symbol (p/q) is ±1 according as p is or is not a nonzero square modulo q; quadratic reciprocity says (p/q)(q/p)=(−1)^(((p−1)/2)((q−1)/2)).

A Derivation, Step by Step

Squares mod 7: 1,4,2,2,4,1 (from 1² through 6²); the residues are {1,2,4} and the nonresidues {3,5,6}, three of each. 3 is a nonresidue mod 7: 3³=27≡6≡−1 (mod 7); by contrast, 7 is a residue mod 3 (7≡1=1²). Reciprocity turns such verdicts into a "symbol game".

Pause and Try It

List all quadratic residues mod 11, and decide whether 11 is a residue mod 5.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

1² through 5² give 1,4,9,5,3 in turn, so the residue set is {1,3,4,5,9}. Since 11≡1 (mod 5) and 1 is plainly a square, 11 is a residue. Quadratic residues are the first step toward quadratic fields and deeper number theory (see the 03 branch page for the route), and they extend the congruence toolbox of the cryptography lesson in 14.
