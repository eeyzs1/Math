Spreading on Networks

Goal for this lesson: explain "Spreading on Networks" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra; requires derivatives from 06 and basic differential equations from 11. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A network SIR model has susceptible, infected and removed nodes, with infection along edges and recovery at rate γ. Structure affects spread, but an infected hub does not necessarily infect every leaf. The friendship paradox is an aggregate sampling effect: choose an edge uniformly in a finite undirected network with at least one edge, then choose an endpoint. Nodes are sampled in proportion to degree, giving mean degree Σdᵢ²/Σdᵢ≥Σdᵢ/N by nonnegative degree variance. It does not say every person's friends have more friends. Choosing a person first and then a neighbor gives a different distribution that must be analyzed separately.

A Derivation, Step by Step

The hub of a star has many transmission routes, but whether each transmission succeeds depends on infection and recovery rates and elapsed time. One round need not infect every leaf. A chain has fewer routes; speed and final outbreak size also depend on the dynamics. Structure describes opportunities, not a replacement for a probability model.

Pause and Try It

Why is 'immunizing the friends of a randomly chosen person' usually more effective than 'immunizing a randomly chosen person'? Explain with the friendship paradox and the handshaking lemma (Lesson 03 of 08).

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The friendship paradox says a randomly chosen friend has a higher average degree; cutting off high-degree nodes first pushes the effective transmission rate below the threshold faster. On networks the epidemic threshold depends on the first and second moments of the degree distribution — graph theory (08), probability (09), and dynamical systems (11) converge here.
