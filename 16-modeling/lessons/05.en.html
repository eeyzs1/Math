Fitting, Extrapolation, and Overfitting

Goal for this lesson: explain "Fitting, Extrapolation, and Overfitting" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra; requires derivatives from 06 and basic differential equations from 11. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Within the data's range a model interpolates; outside it, the model extrapolates — and extrapolation leans on mechanistic assumptions, not on data. Too many parameters overfit: the training error is tiny while performance on new data collapses. The defenses are held-out validation data and comparison against simpler models.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Two points determine a line uniquely; switch to a quadratic fit and infinitely many parabolas pass through — when the number of points equals the number of parameters, a 'perfect fit' is the norm (as long as the x-values are distinct), yet it carries no constraining power. Zero training error is not evidence that a model is right.

Pause and Try It

What is the relation between 'the first half of the data cannot distinguish exponential from logistic' and overfitting?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Both are a shortage of information: the former is structural (for N≪K the two models' predictions coincide, so the data cannot separate the mechanisms), while the latter is parameters outnumbering the data's constraints. The shared remedies: wider-ranging data, an independent validation set, and a preference for simpler mechanistic models. The default tool for fitting parameters is least squares — see Lesson 06 of 10.
