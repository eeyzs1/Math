Transformations and Invariants

Goal for this lesson: explain "Transformations and Invariants" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra, coordinates, and basic proof. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Translations, rotations, and reflections preserve all distances (rigid motions); similarity transformations preserve angles and ratios of lengths; affine transformations preserve parallelism and collinearity but no longer angles. To identify a class of transformations is to find what it preserves — invariants are the core method for classifying geometries (historically, Klein unified the geometries through exactly this viewpoint, leading to the lesson on non-Euclidean geometry in 17).

A Derivation, Step by Step

The shear [[1,1],[0,1]] turns a square into a parallelogram. Its determinant is 1, so it preserves area while changing angles. A general invertible affine map multiplies areas by |det A|: diag(2,1) doubles them. Area itself is therefore not an affine invariant; ratios of areas are. Rotations preserve lengths and angles.

Pause and Try It

Is a parallelogram still a parallelogram after an affine transformation? What about its angles?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Yes. An affine map (an invertible linear part plus a translation) sends parallel lines to parallel lines, so both pairs of opposite sides remain parallel. The angles generally change — the shear is a counterexample. Affine geometry studies precisely the properties that remain beyond angles.
