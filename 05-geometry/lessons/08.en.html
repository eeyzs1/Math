Parametric Equations and Polar Coordinates

Goal for this lesson: explain "Parametric Equations and Polar Coordinates" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra, coordinates, and basic proof. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A curve can be run through a parameter: x=f(t), y=g(t); in physics t is often time, and the velocity points along the tangent. Polar coordinates locate a point by 'distance + direction' (r,θ): x=r·cosθ, y=r·sinθ; circles, spirals, and rose petals take a single line in polar form.

A Derivation, Step by Step

For R>0, the circle x=R cosθ, y=R sinθ satisfies x²+y²=R². Differentiation with respect to θ gives the tangent vector (−R sinθ,R cosθ), orthogonal to the radius vector (R cosθ,R sinθ). Only after normalization is the unit tangent (−sinθ,cosθ).

Pause and Try It

Convert r=2cosθ to Cartesian coordinates and identify the curve.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

r²=2r·cosθ, i.e., x²+y²=2x; completing the square gives (x−1)²+y²=1 — the circle with center (1,0) and radius 1. The same circle looks simpler or messier in different coordinate systems — choosing coordinates is choosing a symmetry.
