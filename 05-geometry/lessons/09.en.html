Parallel Projection and the Cross-Ratio

Goal for this lesson: explain "Parallel Projection and the Cross-Ratio" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra, coordinates, and basic proof. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Projective geometry starts from drawing: points are projected along lines of sight onto a canvas; lengths and angles change, but 'whether points are collinear' does not. Parallel lines meet at a 'point at infinity' on the projective plane — the point on the horizon where the two rails of a railway photograph meet. The cross-ratio (AC/BC)/(AD/BD) of four collinear points A,B,C,D is unchanged by any central projection — the first invariant of projective geometry.

A Derivation, Step by Step

For A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4 the cross-ratio is (2/1)/(3/2)=4/3. Project the whole line by t↦1/t: the four points become 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, and the cross-ratio becomes (2/3)/(1/6)÷(3/4)/(1/4)=4÷3=4/3 — unmoved. All the distances changed, yet the cross-ratio recognizes the quadruple.

Pause and Try It

Compute the cross-ratio for A=0, B=1, C=2, D=4; then for 1, 2, 4, 8. What does the difference between the two values mean?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

(2/1)/(4/3)=3/2; and (3/2)/(7/6)=9/7. The cross-ratio is a 'projective fingerprint' of a quadruple of points: any projection preserves the value for one and the same quadruple, while different quadruples take different values. It plays the role in projective geometry that distance plays in Euclidean geometry (the projective direction on the 05 branch page).
