Parallel Projection and the Cross-Ratio

Goal for this lesson: explain "Parallel Projection and the Cross-Ratio" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra, coordinates, and basic proof. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Projective geometry begins with central projection: lengths and angles can change while collinearity is preserved. Parallel lines in the same direction meet at one point at infinity. For four distinct ordered points on a real projective line, in affine coordinates with nonzero denominators the cross-ratio is ((C−A)/(C−B))/((D−A)/(D−B)). These are directed differences, not arbitrary unsigned distances. Invertible projective transformations preserve the cross-ratio; permuting the ordered points can change it.

A Derivation, Step by Step

For A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4 the cross-ratio is (2/1)/(3/2)=4/3. Project the whole line by t↦1/t: the four points become 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, and the cross-ratio becomes (2/3)/(1/6)÷(3/4)/(1/4)=4÷3=4/3 — unmoved. All the distances changed, yet the cross-ratio recognizes the quadruple.

Pause and Try It

Compute the cross-ratio for A=0, B=1, C=2, D=4; then for 1, 2, 4, 8. What does the difference between the two values mean?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The ordered quadruples have cross-ratios (2/1)/(4/3)=3/2 and (3/2)/(7/6)=9/7. Thus no invertible projective transformation sends the first quadruple to the second in the specified order. Different quadruples can have the same cross-ratio, for example after translating all four points. Distinct quadruples need not have distinct cross-ratios.
