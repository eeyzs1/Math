Numerical Analysis and Scientific Computing / LESSON 01

Floating Point: Why 0.1+0.2 ≠ 0.3

Goal for this lesson: explain "Floating Point" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra and calculus. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Computers represent numbers with finitely many binary digits; the binary expansion of 0.1 is an infinite repeating fraction, so storing it necessarily truncates — a fact about the representation, not a programming bug. Rounding errors accumulate across operations; subtracting two nearly equal numbers causes cancellation and loses many significant digits. Numerical conclusions should report their precision, and equality should be tested with a tolerance rather than exact comparison.

A Derivation, Step by Step

1−0.9999999: the true value is 10⁻⁷, but 0.9999999 itself already carries a representation error on the order of 10⁻¹⁶, and after the subtraction the relative error is amplified enormously. Comparing floating-point numbers should be written |a−b|

Pause and Try It

Summing 1/n for n=1 to 10000 — is it usually more accurate to add from small to large, or from large to small? Why?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Small to large is usually more accurate. Adding the big numbers first swamps the later small ones — every addition's rounding loses information; letting the small terms accumulate into a larger one before joining the sum loses less. In floating point, the order of operations is part of the algorithm.
