Numerical Analysis and Scientific Computing / LESSON 01

Floating Point: Why 0.1+0.2 ≠ 0.3

Goal for this lesson: explain "Floating Point" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra and calculus. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Floating-point numbers usually use a finite binary significand and an exponent. Since 0.1 has no finite binary representation, common IEEE 754 operations round to representable numbers rather than simply truncating. Subtracting nearby approximate values can amplify relative error already present in the inputs; not every subtraction itself incurs a large new rounding error. Compare approximate results with suitable absolute and relative tolerances, for example |a−b|≤atol+rtol·max(|a|,|b|), chosen for the problem scale.

A Derivation, Step by Step

1−0.9999999: the true value is 10⁻⁷, but 0.9999999 itself already carries a representation error on the order of 10⁻¹⁶, and after the subtraction the relative error is amplified enormously. Comparing floating-point numbers should be written |a−b|<tolerance, not a==b.

Pause and Try It

Summing 1/n for n=1 to 10000 — is it usually more accurate to add from small to large, or from large to small? Why?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Small-to-large refers to term values: add 1/10000,1/9999,…,1, rather than increasing the index from 1 to 10000. For these positive terms, combining small values first usually reduces loss when tiny terms are added to a large partial sum. This is not a universal optimality guarantee for signed sequences. Pairwise or compensated summation can also reduce error; operation order is part of a floating-point algorithm.
