Interpolation: The Lagrange Polynomial

Goal for this lesson: explain "Interpolation" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra and calculus. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Through n+1 points with distinct x-coordinates there passes exactly one polynomial of degree at most n. The Lagrange form: the basis function Lᵢ equals 1 at the i-th point and 0 at all the others, and p=ΣyᵢLᵢ. Interpolation is not approximation: high-degree interpolation on equally spaced nodes oscillates near the ends (the Runge phenomenon), so splines switch to low-degree pieces, trading 'lower degree' for stability.

A Derivation, Step by Step

The quadratic through (0,1), (1,3), (2,2) is p(x)=−1.5x²+3.5x+1: check p(0)=1, p(1)=3, p(2)=2. It is assembled from basis functions such as L₀=(x−1)(x−2)/2 — each basis function 'claims' one point and vanishes at the others.

Pause and Try It

What does linear interpolation through (1,2) and (3,5) give at x=2? Is it the average of the two endpoint values?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

p(2)=2+(5−2)/(3−1)×(2−1)=3.5 — exactly the average of the endpoints (a coincidence of the equally spaced midpoint). With three or more points, the interpolant is determined by all the nodes; it is not a pointwise average. Interpolation answers 'passing through', while regression (Lesson 06 of 10) answers 'passing nearest': use interpolation for exact data, regression for noisy data.
