Numerical Analysis and Scientific Computing / LESSON 07

Numerical Linear Algebra: Condition Numbers and Stability

Goal for this lesson: explain "Numerical Linear Algebra" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra and calculus. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Errors in solving Ax=b come from more than rounding: the relative error of the input b can be amplified by as much as the condition number κ(A)=‖A‖·‖A⁻¹‖. A large κ is called ill-conditioned — the problem itself is hard, independent of the algorithm; the algorithm's stability is responsible for 'not ruining a good problem'. Practical points: use pivoting in elimination, and iterative methods (such as conjugate gradient) for large sparse matrices. Standard conjugate gradients require a real symmetric positive-definite matrix; sparsity alone is insufficient.

A Derivation, Step by Step

A=[[1,1],[1,1.0001]], κ≈4×10⁴. The true solution for b=(2,2) is (2,0); perturbing b to (2,2.0001) changes the solution to (1,1) — a perturbation of b on the order of five parts in a hundred thousand completely changes the solution.

Pause and Try It

Using the example above, estimate ‖Δb‖/‖b‖ and ‖Δx‖/‖x‖. By roughly what factor was the error amplified? Is it the same order of magnitude as κ?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Use the Euclidean 2-norm throughout: ‖Δb‖₂/‖b‖₂=0.0001/√8≈3.54×10⁻⁵ and ‖Δx‖₂/‖x‖₂=√2/2≈0.707. The amplification is about 2×10⁴, comparable to and below κ₂(A)≈4×10⁴. Do not mix norms between numerators, denominators or the two sides when checking the bound. A small residual need not imply a small solution error in an ill-conditioned problem.
