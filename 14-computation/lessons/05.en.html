Theoretical Computer Science and Information Mathematics / LESSON 05

RSA: How Number Theory Encrypts

Goal for this lesson: explain "RSA" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Logic, combinatorics, and probability. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Public-key encryption exploits one-way computation: multiplying two large primes is easy, while factoring the product back is far too slow to be feasible. RSA takes n=pq, φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1) (φ is multiplicative over coprime factors), chooses e, and finds d with ed≡1 (mod φ(n)) — finding d is exactly the extended Euclidean algorithm from the lesson on modular inverses in 03. Encryption is c=mᵉ mod n and decryption is m=cᵈ mod n; correctness is guaranteed by Euler's theorem (see 03, Lesson 04).

A Derivation, Step by Step

A toy version: p=3, q=11, n=33, φ=20; take e=7 and d=3 (21≡1 (mod 20)). For the message m=5: 5²=25, 5⁴≡31, 5⁷≡5⁴·5²·5≡31×25×5≡14 (mod 33); decryption gives 14³=2744≡5 (mod 33).

Pause and Try It

Why does "factoring n" amount to "breaking RSA"? Which steps lead from n=pq to recovering d?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Factoring yields p and q; compute φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1), then solve ed≡1 (mod φ) for d. The whole secret sits behind the single door of "factoring is hard" — real systems use primes hundreds of digits long, making this step computationally infeasible. Every piece of the number theory course (primes, Euler, modular inverses) assembles here into one complete application.
