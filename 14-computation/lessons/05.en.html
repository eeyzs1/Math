Theoretical Computer Science and Information Mathematics / LESSON 05

RSA: How Number Theory Encrypts

Goal for this lesson: explain "RSA" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Logic, combinatorics, and probability. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Classroom RSA uses distinct primes p,q, n=pq and φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1). Choose e coprime to φ(n) and use extended Euclid to find ed≡1 (mod φ(n)). A message representative satisfies 0≤m<n; encryption is c=mᵉ mod n and decryption is cᵈ mod n. Euler's theorem proves correctness for m coprime to n; for arbitrary m, prove it modulo p and q and combine by the Chinese remainder theorem. Factoring is one known attack route; RSA inversion has not been proved equivalent to factoring. This is deterministic raw RSA arithmetic; actual encryption schemes need specified encodings such as RSAES-OAEP.

A Derivation, Step by Step

A toy version: p=3, q=11, n=33, φ=20; take e=7 and d=3 (21≡1 (mod 20)). For the message m=5: 5²=25, 5⁴≡31, 5⁷≡5⁴·5²·5≡31×25×5≡14 (mod 33); decryption gives 14³=2744≡5 (mod 33).

Pause and Try It

Why does "factoring n" amount to "breaking RSA"? Which steps lead from n=pq to recovering d?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Factoring n yields p,q and hence φ(n); invert the public e modulo φ(n) to recover a private exponent d. Thus factoring is sufficient to recover a private key, not a proof that every attack must first factor n. Raw RSA's determinism, encoding errors and implementation flaws can also expose messages. The toy example connects primes, modular inverses, Euler and CRT; it is not a deployable encryption scheme.
