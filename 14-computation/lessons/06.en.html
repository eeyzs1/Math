Theoretical Computer Science and Information Mathematics / LESSON 06

Error-Correcting Codes: From Repetition to Hamming

Goal for this lesson: explain "Error-Correcting Codes" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Logic, combinatorics, and probability. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

An error-correcting code uses a subset of legal words and decodes by distance. The repetition code {000,111} corrects at most one flipped bit at rate 1/3. In the even-parity Hamming (7,4) code, parity positions 1,2,4 check {1,3,5,7}, {2,3,6,7}, {4,5,6,7}. If their violation flags are (s₁,s₂,s₄), the error position is s₁+2s₂+4s₄, or binary (s₄s₂s₁)₂. This guarantee assumes at most one bit error; a zero syndrome does not rule out multiple errors.

A Derivation, Step by Step

If (s₁,s₂,s₄)=(1,0,1), the error position is 1+0+4=5. To check bit order, use (1,1,0): the position is 1+2=3, binary 011, not 110 read as 6. Under a single-bit error, flipping that position restores the word. The information rate is 4/7.

Pause and Try It

The repetition code receives 010: which codeword is decided on? Why is it powerless against two bits flipping at once?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

It is at distance 1 from 000 and distance 2 from 111, so decode to 000. If two bits are wrong (say 011), it is closer to 111 instead and the decision flips — error-correcting power is governed by the minimum distance between codewords: distance 3 corrects 1 error, and correcting t errors requires distance 2t+1. Coding theory is where information theory (Lesson 02) converges with linear algebra.
