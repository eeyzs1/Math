Metric Spaces

Goal for this lesson: explain "Metric Spaces" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: sets, maps; advanced topics require analysis and algebra. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A metric space is a set X with a finite real-valued distance d:X×X→[0,∞), satisfying d(x,y)=0 if and only if x=y, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. Examples include Euclidean spaces and the discrete metric. On C([a,b]), continuous functions on a fixed compact interval, d(f,g)=max_{x∈[a,b]}|f(x)−g(x)| is also a metric; compactness ensures a finite maximum. The same max formula cannot be used indiscriminately on unbounded domains.

A Derivation, Step by Step

The sequence of functions fₙ(x)=xⁿ converges pointwise on [0,1] to a discontinuous limit (0 for x<1, 1 for x=1); under the sup metric it converges to no continuous function — the sup metric catches a failure that pointwise convergence cannot see.

Pause and Try It

Under the discrete metric (distinct points at distance 1), which sequences converge?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Only the sequences eventually constant at some point: if xₙ→x while xₙ≠x occurs infinitely often, then d(xₙ,x)=1 does not tend to 0. Here every ball of radius less than 1 contains only its center, so every subset is open — keep the same set but change the metric, and the topology can be completely different.
