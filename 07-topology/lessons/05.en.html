Topology / LESSON 05

Compactness

Goal for this lesson: explain "Compactness" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: sets, maps; advanced topics require analysis and algebra. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Compactness is defined by the requirement that every open cover has a finite subcover; in Rⁿ the Heine–Borel theorem characterizes it as "bounded and closed". A continuous function on a compact set must attain a maximum and a minimum (Extreme Value Theorem) and must be uniformly continuous — a property of "infinitely many points" is compressed into something "checkable in finitely many steps".

A Derivation, Step by Step

[0,1] is compact: x(1−x) attains its maximum 1/4 at 1/2. The open interval (0,1) is not compact: 1/x is unbounded on it, and the open cover {(1/n,1)} has no finite subcover.

Pause and Try It

Why is [0,∞) not compact? Give a continuous function on it that is unbounded.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

f(x)=x is unbounded; the cover {[0,n) : n≥1} has no finite subcover. [0,∞) is closed, but boundedness fails. Compactness is the currency that exchanges "continuity" for "extreme values exist".
