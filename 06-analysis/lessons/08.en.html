Calculus and Mathematical Analysis / LESSON 08

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Goal for this lesson: explain "The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: functions, algebra, coordinates. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Assume first that f is continuous on [a,b]. The first part of the fundamental theorem of calculus says F(x)=∫ₐˣ f(t)dt is differentiable in the interior and F′(x)=f(x). The second says that if G′=f, then ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx=G(b)−G(a). Continuity is the sufficient hypothesis used here; an arbitrary integrand does not automatically give F′=f at every point. Jump points need separate analysis.

A Derivation, Step by Step

∫₀²x dx: F(x)=x²/2 is an antiderivative, and F(2)−F(0)=2, matching the area of the triangle. Integrating velocity gives displacement, and differentiating brings velocity back — each half does its own job.

Pause and Try It

What is ∫₋₁¹|x|dx? Using x²/2 as the antiderivative directly yields 0 — where is the mistake?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

An antiderivative of |x| is the piecewise function F(x)=−x²/2 (x<0), x²/2 (x≥0), and F(1)−F(−1)=1/2−(−1/2)=1. x²/2 is an antiderivative of x, not of |x|; once the integrand changes, a new antiderivative must be found, and handling it piecewise is the norm.
