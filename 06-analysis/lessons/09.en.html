A First Look at Partial Derivatives and the Gradient

Goal for this lesson: explain "A First Look at Partial Derivatives and the Gradient" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: functions, algebra, coordinates. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

z=f(x,y) maps two independent variables to one value; ∂f/∂x means freeze y and differentiate with respect to x alone (∂f/∂y likewise). The gradient ∇f=(∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y) points in the direction of fastest ascent, and its length is the steepest slope. The two-variable chain rule: in u(x,t)=f(x−ct), regard s=x−ct as the intermediate variable; then ∂u/∂t=−c·f′(s) and ∂²u/∂t²=c²f″(s).

A Derivation, Step by Step

f(x,y)=x²y: ∂f/∂x=2xy and ∂f/∂y=x², so ∇f(1,2)=(4,1). For u=f(x−ct): ∂²u/∂t²=c²f″ and ∂²u/∂x²=f″, hence u_tt=c²u_xx — the verification of the wave equation lesson in 15 Mathematical Physics done in one step.

Pause and Try It

Find the gradient of f(x,y)=x·e^y, and identify, at the point (1,0), the direction of fastest ascent and the steepest slope.

Show the solution and self-check criteria

∇f=(e^y, x·e^y), which at (1,0) is (1,1): ascent is fastest in the 45° direction, with slope ‖∇f‖=√2. "Freezing one variable" is the first move of multivariable calculus; the whole intuition of single-variable derivatives is carried over dimension by dimension.
