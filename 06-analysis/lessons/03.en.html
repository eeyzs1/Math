Differentiation Rules and the Mean Value Theorem

Goal for this lesson: explain "Differentiation Rules and the Mean Value Theorem" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: functions, algebra, coordinates. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

The arithmetic operations and composition carry differentiability along: product (uv)′=u′v+uv′, quotient (u/v)′=(u′v−uv′)/v², chain (f(g(x)))′=f′(g)·g′ — under composition, rates of change multiply. Basic derivatives: (xⁿ)′=nxⁿ⁻¹, (eˣ)′=eˣ, (ln x)′=1/x, (sin x)′=cos x. Mean Value Theorem: if f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), then there exists c with f′(c)=[f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a) — the average rate of change actually occurs at some point.

A Derivation, Step by Step

d/dt(100e^(0.1t))=10e^(0.1t): the derivative of the exponential plus the chain rule (the 0.1 sits in the inner layer). d/dt(x′(t)²)=2x′x″: a square on the outside, x′ on the inside — these are exactly the two rules behind E′=mx′x″+kxx′ in the energy lesson of 15 Mathematical Physics.

Pause and Try It

Use the Mean Value Theorem to prove that a function whose derivative is identically zero is constant, and explain what this has to do with potential energy V being fixed only up to a constant.
