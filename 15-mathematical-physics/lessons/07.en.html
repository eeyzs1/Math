Standing Waves, Boundaries, and Discrete Frequencies

Goal for this lesson: explain "Standing Waves, Boundaries, and Discrete Frequencies" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Calculus, linear algebra, and differential equations. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

With both ends of the string fixed (u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0), the wave equation admits only sinusoidal modes whose "nodes fall exactly at the ends": uₙ(x,t)=sin(nπx/L)·cos(nπct/L). The boundary conditions sift the continuum of wavelengths into a discrete spectrum — frequencies can only be integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. This is a mathematical preview of "confinement leads to quantization": energy levels, like frequencies, are sifted out by boundary conditions.

A Derivation, Step by Step

For a string with L=1: the fundamental angular frequency πc and the second harmonic 2πc. If the initial shape is exactly f(x)=sin(πx), only the fundamental mode is excited — superposition of the traveling waves reflected at the two ends (the d'Alembert viewpoint) yields the same conclusion.

Pause and Try It

Why can a string fixed at both ends not carry a "half-wavelength standing wave" of the sin(πx/(2L)) type?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

At x=L it takes the value sin(π/2)=1≠0, violating the boundary condition. "Which solutions exist" is decided by the interval and the boundary values, not by dynamical preference — turning "which frequencies are allowed" into a geometric question is a standard move of mathematical physics (the gateway to the mathematics of quantum theory).
