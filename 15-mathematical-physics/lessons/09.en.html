Mathematical Physics / LESSON 09

The Galilean Transformation and Relativity

Goal for this lesson: explain "The Galilean Transformation and Relativity" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Calculus, linear algebra, and differential equations. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

In a reference frame translated with velocity v along the x direction, the coordinates are replaced by x′=x−vt, t′=t — written as a matrix, a shear-type linear mixing of (x,t). The Galilean principle of relativity: the laws of mechanics take the same form in all inertial frames (experiments in a cabin moving at constant velocity detect nothing of the ship's speed). But the speed of light c delivered by Maxwell's electromagnetic theory does not obey "velocities add and subtract" — the contradiction was finally resolved by special relativity: replace it with the Lorentz transformation x′=γ(x−vt), γ=1/√(1−v²/c²), and time is no longer absolute.

A Derivation, Step by Step

A ball thrown at speed u aboard the ship has speed u+v as seen from the shore; light, whatever the speed of its source, is always c — the clash between these two kinds of "velocity composition" points straight to the revolution of 1905.

Pause and Try It

Under the Galilean transformation, how do the distance between two points at one instant, the time interval, and velocity each transform? Is "simultaneity" still absolute under the Lorentz transformation?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Distance (measured simultaneously) and time interval are unchanged; velocities compose as u′=u−v. Under the Lorentz transformation "simultaneity" depends on the reference frame — absolute time is a low-speed approximation, not a logical necessity. The 2×2 matrices (see 04, Lesson 03) carry all of the kinematics here.
