Mathematical Physics / LESSON 10

The Continuum: From Strings to Fluids

Goal for this lesson: explain "The Continuum" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Calculus, linear algebra, and differential equations. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A string is a continuum with its matter compressed into one dimension: each position x carries a displacement u(x,t), and local tension plus Newton's laws yields the wave equation. The same recipe climbs in dimension: a fluid is described by a density ρ(x,t) and a velocity field v(x,t); conservation of mass gives ∂ρ/∂t+∇·(ρv)=0, and conservation of momentum gives the Euler/Navier–Stokes equations; sound is precisely the propagation of pressure disturbances — a close cousin of the wave equation. The shared technique is linearization: for small disturbances, discard the higher-order terms.

A Derivation, Step by Step

String: tension provides the restoring force → u_tt=c²u_xx. Gas: a compressed region pushes on its surroundings → the pressure disturbance satisfies p_tt=c²p_xx, where c is the speed of sound (about 340 m/s in air). One mathematical skeleton (a hyperbolic equation) carries strings, sound, and water waves.

Pause and Try It

Why is conservation of mass written as ∂ρ/∂t+∇·(ρv)=0? What is ∫ρv·n dA?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

For a fixed region: the rate at which the interior mass decreases = the net rate of outflow; ρv is the mass flux density, the surface integral ∫ρv·n dA is the mass leaving through the boundary per unit time, the divergence theorem turns it into a volume integral, and rearranging gives the equation. Conservation law + localization = the standard generator of partial differential equations (see the 15 and 11 branch pages for the continuum route).
