Least Squares Regression

Goal for this lesson: explain "Least Squares Regression" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Probability and algebra; binomial coefficients: see 08. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Fit the line y=a+bx to the scatter of points (xᵢ,yᵢ), choosing a and b to minimize the sum of squared residuals Σ(yᵢ−a−bxᵢ)². The two normal equations give a closed form: b=Cov(x,y)/Var(x) (covariance: see 09, Lesson 06) and a=ȳ−bx̄. R² is the proportion of the variance explained by the regression. The geometric interpretation of least squares is "projection onto the column space": see 04, Lesson 08. The slope formula requires Var(x)>0. With an intercept and nonconstant responses, R²=1−residual sum of squares/total sum of squares. This interpretation cannot be transferred indiscriminately to models without an intercept.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Points (1,2),(2,3),(3,5): x̄=2, ȳ=10/3, Cov=1, Var(x)=2/3, giving b=1.5 and a=1/3, so the line is y=1/3+1.5x.

Pause and Try It

Why minimize the "squares" instead of the absolute values?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Squared loss is smooth, and this linear model has a unique closed-form fit when the design matrix has full column rank. It also corresponds to maximum likelihood under independent homoscedastic Gaussian errors. Absolute loss is nondifferentiable at zero residual and usually more robust to extremes; general linear regression requires different optimization methods. It is false that absolute loss never has a closed-form solution: for a constant-only fit, any median minimizes it.
