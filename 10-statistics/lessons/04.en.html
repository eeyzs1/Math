Statistics / LESSON 04

Point Estimation and Confidence Intervals

Goal for this lesson: explain "Point Estimation and Confidence Intervals" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Probability and algebra; binomial coefficients: see 08. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A parameter is estimated with a statistic (such as the sample mean); unbiasedness means that the expectation of the estimator equals the true value. When the population is normal or the sample size is large enough (by the central limit theorem, see 09, Lesson 07) and σ is known, X̄±1.96σ/√n is a 95% confidence interval for μ (1.96 is a quantile of the normal distribution, see 09, Lesson 05). The correct reading is "in repeated sampling, 95% of intervals of this kind cover μ", not "the probability that μ lies in this interval is 95%" — μ is not random.

A Derivation, Step by Step

With n=25, sample mean 10, and σ=5: the interval is 10±1.96×5/√25=10±1.96, that is, (8.04, 11.96).

Pause and Try It

What goes wrong if you plug in 1.96 directly when σ is unknown and the sample is small?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

It understates the uncertainty: σ must instead be estimated by the sample standard deviation s (computed from the sample variance that divides by n−1, which corrects the systematic underestimate of the "divide by n" convention used in this lesson), and substituting s for σ brings extra variability, so the quantile should be replaced by a larger value from the t distribution; as n grows, t approaches the normal. The width of the interval is the price of reporting uncertainty honestly.
