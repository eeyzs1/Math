Introduction to Bayesian Statistics

Goal for this lesson: explain "Introduction to Bayesian Statistics" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Probability and algebra; binomial coefficients: see 08. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Frequentists treat the parameter as a fixed but unknown quantity and reason through sampling distributions; Bayesians treat the parameter as a random variable: assign a prior distribution and update it with the data to obtain the posterior — posterior∝likelihood×prior. Bayes' rule in 09 is the single-event version; here the update operates on a whole distribution over the parameter θ. The prior is a subjective input written out in the open; it can be examined and subjected to sensitivity analysis — which is why it is both criticized and prized.

A Derivation, Step by Step

For the heads probability θ of a coin: take the prior Beta(2,2) (centered, with a slight preference for fairness). After observing 8 heads and 2 tails, the posterior is Beta(10,4) with mean 10/14≈0.71 — ten observations pull the prior markedly toward the data.

Pause and Try It

With prior Beta(1,1) (the uniform distribution) and 3 heads against 1 tail observed, what is the posterior mean? And what if a further 20 tosses give 15 heads and 5 tails?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

From Beta(1,1), observing 3 heads and 1 tail gives Beta(4,2), mean 4/6=2/3. Adding 15 heads and 5 tails yields Beta(19,7), mean 19/26≈0.73. In this conjugate model, the relative weight of a fixed prior's pseudocounts decreases with sample size. General asymptotic agreement with frequentist methods needs model, identifiability and prior-support conditions; it is not guaranteed for arbitrary priors or models.
