Correlation Alone Does Not Prove Causation

Goal for this lesson: explain "Correlation Alone Does Not Prove Causation" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Probability and algebra; binomial coefficients: see 08. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

When two quantities move together, the explanation may be causation, a common cause, or a selection mechanism; for a modeling perspective on confounders, see the 16 Modeling branch. A randomized controlled trial uses random assignment to balance confounding factors as far as possible; its execution and its sample still have to be checked.

A Derivation, Step by Step

In summer, ice cream sales and the number of drownings may rise together. Hotter weather makes people buy ice cream and go swimming more often, so temperature is a common cause. Banning ice cream on the strength of the correlation alone does not imply that drownings would decrease.

Pause and Try It

Coffee drinkers score higher on exams — can we conclude directly that coffee raises scores?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

No. Study time, sleep, and sample selection can all confound. First pin down the causal question, then consider a randomized experiment or a suitably designed observational study.

Self-check: Is the conclusion correct? Are the conditions stated? Can you explain the key steps without looking at the example? If you only recalled the answer, redo the lesson.
