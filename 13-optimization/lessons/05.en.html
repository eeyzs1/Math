Optimization, Operations Research, and Control / LESSON 05

Duality and KKT: Viewing the Optimum from the Other Side

Goal for this lesson: explain "Duality and KKT" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra; later lessons require calculus and linear algebra. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Every linear program min cᵀx (constraints Ax≥b, x≥0) has a dual max bᵀy (constraints Aᵀy≤c, y≥0); weak duality says feasible solutions on either side bound each other, strong duality says the optimal values coincide. The optimal dual variable yᵢ is a 'shadow price': loosening the right-hand side of constraint i by one unit improves the objective by yᵢ. The first-order conditions for a nonlinear problem with inequality constraints are the KKT conditions: the objective's gradient is canceled by a multiplier combination of the constraint gradients, with the multipliers paired with complementary slackness — a generalization of the method of Lagrange multipliers, and the gateway to the calculus of variations and optimal control.

A Derivation, Step by Step

The dual of max x+y s.t. x+2y≤4, x,y≥0 (Lesson 04) is min 4w s.t. w≥1, 2w≥1, w≥0, with optimum w=1 and value 4 — equal to the primal optimum 4. The same mountain, climbed from two sides.

Pause and Try It

In the example above, loosen the right-hand side 4 to 4.5. Roughly how much does the optimal value change? Does it agree with the shadow price w=1?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The optimal vertex moves from (4,0) to (4.5,0) and the value from 4 to 4.5 — an increment of 0.5=1×0.5, exactly w×Δb. Within the range where the constraint remains active, the shadow price forecasts the sensitivity exactly; reading the dual variable is reading 'what the constraint is worth'.
