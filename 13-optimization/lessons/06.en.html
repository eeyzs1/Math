Game Theory: Nash Equilibrium

Goal for this lesson: explain "Game Theory" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra; later lessons require calculus and linear algebra. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

A game = players, strategy sets, and payoff functions. A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile with 'no profitable unilateral deviation': each player's choice is a best response to the others' strategies. A pure-strategy equilibrium may fail to exist (rock–paper–scissors), but Nash's theorem guarantees that every finite game has a mixed-strategy equilibrium. Equilibrium does not mean collective optimality: in the prisoner's dilemma the equilibrium is mutual defection, worse than mutual cooperation — the crack between individual and collective rationality.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Prisoner's dilemma: both stay silent → 1 year each; one defects → the defector walks free and the other serves 10 years; both defect → 5 years each. Whatever the other does, defecting is lighter (0<1 and 5<10), so (defect, defect) is the unique equilibrium — 5 years each, worse than the 1 year each under mutual silence.

Pause and Try It

Matching pennies (isomorphic to rock–paper–scissors): what is each side's optimal mixed strategy? Why is the expected payoff 0?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

Each randomizes heads/tails with probability 1/2 — any bias would be exploited by the opponent. A symmetric game with both sides best-responding forces an expected payoff of 0. Games with no pure-strategy stable point protect themselves by randomizing: the equilibrium concept shifts from 'optimal solution' to 'no one wants to deviate unilaterally' — the starting point of the mathematics of interactive decision-making (see the 13 branch page for the route).
