Why Gradient Descent Can Overshoot

Goal for this lesson: explain "Why Gradient Descent Can Overshoot" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Algebra; later lessons require calculus and linear algebra. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

For a differentiable function, the gradient gives the direction of steepest local ascent; this lesson stays one-dimensional (the multivariable gradient appears in Lesson 09 of 06, and convergence in several dimensions is governed by the eigenvalues of the second-derivative matrix — see Lesson 07 of 04). The descent iteration reads x_new=x−ηf′(x), with a positive step size η. It is not magic that guarantees a global optimum for every function.

A Derivation, Step by Step

For f(x)=x² the iteration becomes x_new=(1−2η)x. If 0<η<1, the absolute factor is below 1, so the iterates tend to 0; at η=1 they merely bounce back and forth; for η>1 they typically diverge.

Pause and Try It

With η=0.25 and x₀=4, what are the first two iterates?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The factor is 1/2, so x₁=2 and x₂=1. The function value drops from 16 to 4 and then to 1. The convergence condition comes from this specific quadratic.
