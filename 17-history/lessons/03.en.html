Infinitesimals and the Birth of Limits

Goal for this lesson: explain "Infinitesimals and the Birth of Limits" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Curiosity and basic algebra. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

The 'infinitesimals' of Newton and Leibniz worked but were logically murky: divide by an increment first, then let the increment vanish — self-contradictory in sequence (Berkeley mocked them as 'the ghosts of departed quantities'). In the nineteenth century Cauchy began, and Weierstrass completed, the reconstruction of limits with ε-δ, translating 'approaches arbitrarily closely' into a finite language of inequalities (the formal definition appears in Lesson 01 of 06).

A Derivation, Step by Step

To find the fluxion of x², one first computes (x+o)²−x², divides by o to get 2x+o, and then 'lets o be zero' to obtain 2x — the increment is first treated as nonzero and then as zero, precisely the point under attack. The ε-δ definition never lets the increment 'become zero'; it only requires |f(x)−L|<ε whenever 0<|x−a|<δ — note the punctured condition excluding x=a, exactly where the difference quotient has no definition.

Pause and Try It

Does the ε-δ definition answer 'what is an infinitesimal', or does it bypass the question?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

It bypasses rather than answers: it stops talking about infinitely small quantities and expresses 'as close as desired' using only the finite positive numbers ε and δ. This is a successful translation — restating an intractable infinite object as a checkable finite statement; mathematics often substitutes reformulation for frontal assault.
