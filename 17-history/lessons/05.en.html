Philosophy of Mathematics: Three Schools and Incompleteness

Goal for this lesson: explain "Philosophy of Mathematics" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Curiosity and basic algebra. Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Logicism (Frege, Russell) sought to reduce mathematics to logic; formalism (Hilbert) emphasized formal systems and consistency proofs; intuitionism (Brouwer) emphasized constructions and restricted unrestricted excluded middle. Incompleteness has essential hypotheses: a consistent, effectively axiomatized system expressing enough basic arithmetic cannot decide every sentence in its language. The second theorem, with the usual provability-coding conditions, limits proving its own consistency internally. Not every consistent system is incomplete, nor is a sentence unprovable in every stronger system.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Gödel encoded 'statements about proofs' as natural numbers (Gödel numbering) and then used the diagonal to construct 'this proposition is unprovable' — the same method as Cantor's diagonal (Lesson 07 of 01) and the halting problem (Lesson 04 of 14).

Pause and Try It

How does 'axioms are chosen starting points' (Lesson 02) connect with the disagreements among the three schools? What does incompleteness show?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The schools differ over mathematical objects and acceptable proofs; arbitrary consistent axioms alone do not capture those differences. Gödel's conclusion applies to effectively axiomatized theories strong enough for arithmetic: under those conditions, consistency and sentence-completeness cannot coexist. Without effective axiomatizability, true arithmetic is a consistent complete theory, but its axioms cannot be effectively enumerated. The theorem limits particular formal methods rather than mathematical proof itself.
