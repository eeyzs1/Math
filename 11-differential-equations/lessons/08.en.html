Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems / LESSON 08

Bifurcations: How a Parameter Changes the Phase Portrait

Goal for this lesson: explain "Bifurcations" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Derivatives and integrals; Lesson 7 on the phase plane requires linear algebra (eigenvalues: see 04, Lesson 07). Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

In a system with a parameter, x′=f(x;r), an event where the qualitative structure (the number of equilibria and their stability) changes abruptly as r varies is called a bifurcation. The simplest saddle-node bifurcation is x′=r−x²: for r<0 there is no equilibrium and trajectories slide all the way down; at r=0 a semi-stable equilibrium appears at 0; for r>0 it splits into two equilibria, one stable (√r) and one unstable (−√r). A bifurcation diagram plots the equilibria as curves against r: a solid curve for the stable branch, a dashed curve for the unstable branch.

A Derivation, Step by Step

r=1: the equilibria are −1 (f′=−2x=2>0, unstable) and 1 (f′=−2, stable). As the parameter sweeps from positive to negative, the two equilibria collide head-on at r=0 and annihilate each other — at the critical value the system has "nowhere to stop".

Pause and Try It

For x′=rx−x² (the logistic equation with a parameter), how does the structure of equilibria change as r goes from negative to positive?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The equilibria are 0 and r. For r<0: 0 is stable (f′(0)=r<0), while r is negative and unstable; for r>0: 0 loses stability (f′(0)=r>0), while r turns positive and stable — a transcritical bifurcation: the two equilibria exchange stability at r=0, and as the parameter crosses the critical value the system (on the half-line x≥0) switches from "collapsing to zero" to "sustaining itself". This is where the logistic equation of Lesson 04 meets the logistic model of 16 Modeling.
