Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems / LESSON 07

The Phase Plane and Linear Systems

Goal for this lesson: explain "The Phase Plane and Linear Systems" in your own words, then complete the transfer exercise below on your own.

Prerequisites: Derivatives and integrals; Lesson 7 on the phase plane requires linear algebra (eigenvalues: see 04, Lesson 07). Try the worked example first; if the notation feels heavy, go back to the preparation module.

Intuition First, Then Symbols

Two coupled first-order equations, x′=ax+by and y′=cx+dy, combine into v′=Av; plotting (x,y) at each instant in the phase plane gives the trajectories. The origin is an equilibrium, and its type is decided by the eigenvalues: two negative real numbers = a stable node; one positive and one negative = a saddle point; complex numbers with negative real part = a stable spiral; purely imaginary numbers = a center (closed orbits). This is the gateway where "dynamical systems" begins to live up to its name, and the linear algebra promised in the prerequisites is cashed in here.

A Derivation, Step by Step

Simple harmonic oscillation x″=−4x turns into x′=v, v′=−4x, i.e. A=[[0,1],[−4,0]], whose characteristic equation λ²+4=0 gives λ=±2i: a center whose trajectories are concentric ellipses — the geometric image of conservation of energy (the lesson on simple harmonic oscillation in 15 Mathematical Physics).

Pause and Try It

What shape are the trajectories of A=diag(1,−1) (that is, x′=x, y′=−y)? What type is the origin?

Show the solution and self-check criteria

The solutions are x=c₁eᵗ and y=c₂e^(−t), so the product xy=c₁c₂ is constant: a family of hyperbolas, and the origin is a saddle point. Trajectories flee along one eigen-direction and approach along the other — the signs of the eigenvalues let you read off the entire qualitative story.
